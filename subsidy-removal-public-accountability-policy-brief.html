From Subsidy Removal to Public Accountability: Making Nigeria's Fuel Reform Work for Households by 2030

Author Muhammad Garba Isah
Affiliation Department of International Relations, Umaru Musa Yar'Adua University, Katsina State, Nigeria — B.Sc. International Relations (undergraduate, expected 2028).
Contact gnep.partnership@gmail.com (Editorial Correspondence)
Publication Type Independent Policy Analysis
Data Current As Of October 2026. Principal sources: Federal Ministry of Finance, Nigeria's Reform Scorecard (19 August 2026); World Bank Nigeria Development Update (December 2023; September 2026 Implementation Status Report); National Bureau of Statistics, Premium Motor Spirit Price Watch (monthly, through May 2026).
This brief reflects the author's independent analysis and does not represent the position of any affiliated organization.

Executive Summary

Nigeria's May 2023 subsidy removal, alongside foreign-exchange unification, was followed by a 163% average rise in retail petrol prices reported in the World Bank's December 2023 update (World Bank, 2023). The Federal Ministry of Finance reports that the combined reforms mobilised ₦15.8 trillion for the Federation by December 2025, but only ₦5.43 trillion reached the Federal Government, and new borrowing supplied 58% of its incremental resources (Oyedele, 2026; Proshare, 2026). For households the record is mixed: headline inflation fell, but by mid-2025 the cash-transfer programme had reached only 37% of its target households (World Bank, 2025), and ₦33.75 billion in 2023 transfers could not be verified (Auditor-General for the Federation, 2024). Nigeria has built fiscal space but not yet the reporting to show that it reaches households. By mid-2027, the Ministry of Finance and the National Cash Transfer Office should publish quarterly, disaggregated fiscal and social reports reconciled to audited accounts, as Section 30 of the Fiscal Responsibility Act 2007 already requires.

What Was Done

On 29 May 2023, President Bola Ahmed Tinubu announced the end of Nigeria's petrol subsidy regime, effective from his inauguration that day; the Federal Government's own accounting of the reform's fiscal effect dates the operative period from June 2023 (Tinubu, 2023; Oyedele, 2026). The administration paired this with the unification of Nigeria's multiple foreign-exchange windows shortly afterward. Because the two reforms were announced within weeks of each other and are tracked together in the government's own reporting, this brief treats their combined, rather than separately attributable, fiscal and household effects — a limitation discussed further below.

Before the removal, the World Bank described the subsidy as costly (2.2% of GDP in 2022), opaque, and regressive (World Bank, 2023, slide 6). A separate World Bank estimate put the combined cost of the petrol subsidy and multiple exchange rates at about ₦10 trillion in 2022 (World Bank, 2024). The Nigerian Economic Summit Group put the subsidy's cost at approximately 31.7% of projected 2022 federal government revenue (NESG, 2022) — a different metric, on a different revenue base, from the World Bank's GDP-share figure; the two are not directly comparable, and this brief cites each to its own source.

The World Bank described “the bold reforms” as having stopped Nigeria's slide towards a crisis (World Bank, 2023, slide 4), while also stating that, though needed, the reforms “entail large, painful adjustments” (World Bank, 2023, slide 9). Both halves of that assessment matter for how the reform should be judged.

The central policy question is therefore not whether the subsidy imposed a fiscal burden — the evidence above shows clearly that it did — but whether the resulting gains are being managed transparently and translated into measurable benefit for Nigerians.

Did It Work?

In short: fiscally, the reform has generated real resources, though a large share of the federal government's effective gain still rests on new borrowing rather than subsidy savings alone. For households, the evidence of durable benefit is not yet established.

Fiscal Outcomes

According to the Federal Ministry of Finance's August 2026 Reform Scorecard, the reforms mobilised ₦15.8 trillion in resources for the Federation between June 2023 and December 2025 (Oyedele, 2026). Of this, ₦5.43 trillion accrued to the Federal Government, ₦6.52 trillion to the 36 states, and ₦3.88 trillion to local governments (Oyedele, 2026; Proshare, 2026). States and local governments together received almost two-thirds of the total — a point this brief's recommendations address directly, since subnational accountability for these funds has received far less public scrutiny than the federal share.

The Federal Government's wider resource envelope, and how it compares with what the government actually spent, is set out below (Figure 1).

Two things follow from this table that the government's own framing does not foreground. First, the ₦10.24 trillion gap between incremental resources and incremental expenditure was not unexplained overspending — it was funded from the government's existing, pre-reform baseline revenue (Proshare, 2026). Second, a single category — wage adjustments and the exchange-rate effect on external debt service — absorbed ₦18.75 trillion, or 61.2% of total incremental spending, more than three times the Federal Government's entire subsidy-linked share (Proshare, 2026). This is the clearest evidence in the government's own figures that the subsidy savings, taken alone, financed only a modest fraction of the fiscal adjustment the country actually undertook. As the Ministry of Finance has itself stated, the ₦15.8 trillion figure is not a separate pool of saved cash sitting in an account; it is a figure embedded in a wider financing system that depended heavily on new borrowing (Oyedele, 2026). This brief adopts the government's own caveat rather than overriding it: these are reported figures, not independently verified outcomes, and should be read as such throughout. One further caution: Proshare (2026) reports the ₦30.64 trillion as incremental (“additional”) expenditure, whereas some press coverage describes it as total spending over the period; the distinction should be confirmed against the scorecard document itself before the ratios above are relied on.

A further limitation bears directly on how this reform should be assessed. The ₦15.8 trillion figure is the joint product of subsidy removal and foreign-exchange unification, and the Minister of Finance has attributed the combined gain to both reforms together (Oyedele, 2026). The World Bank's December 2023 update likewise noted that FX unification had created a windfall for Federation revenues, while gains from subsidy removal were lower than expected (World Bank, 2023, slide 19). Published figures do not allow the two effects to be cleanly separated. This is not a minor caveat — it is, in this brief's assessment, the single strongest argument for the disaggregated reporting this brief recommends.

Regional Price Variation

A geographically uneven burden is often assumed in public discussion of this reform, on the premise that landlocked northern states farther from fuel depots and ports bear structurally higher transport costs. The National Bureau of Statistics' monthly Premium Motor Spirit Price Watch does confirm real zonal price variation — but not a stable north-south pattern. In February 2026, the North-East recorded the country's highest zonal average (₦1,084.41) and the South-West the lowest (₦1,023.89); by April and May 2026, the pattern had reversed, with the South-South recording the highest average (₦1,566.76 and ₦1,623.84 respectively) and the North-West the lowest (₦1,508.81 and ₦1,564.11) (National Bureau of Statistics, 2026a, 2026b, 2026c).

This volatility is itself the finding worth reporting, rather than any single snapshot. Rather than supporting a claim that northern households face a permanently larger burden, the data show that which zone pays most shifts from month to month — more consistent with fluctuating distribution costs, depot access, and local logistics than with a fixed geographic disadvantage. A credible household-burden claim would require expenditure-share or poverty data (for instance, from an NBS household survey) showing how much of a typical household's budget fuel represents in each zone, not pump-price comparisons alone. This brief does not have that data and does not make that claim; the regional-equity question is better addressed through the state-level transparency recommended below than through a single headline price comparison.

Household and Social-Protection Outcomes

Fiscal reform of this scale is conventionally expected to be paired with protection for the households most exposed to it. Nigeria's principal instrument has been the conditional cash transfer programme, launched on 17 October 2023 to reach 15 million poor, near-poor and vulnerable households living on less than ₦75,000 a month, with ₦25,000 per month for three months paid electronically (World Bank, 2023, slide 13). The World Bank approved an $800 million loan to support it (World Bank, 2025).

Delivery has lagged the targets throughout. At the World Bank's December 2023 update, 1.5 million households — about 10% of the 15-million target — had received money, against an expectation of 5 million by the end of December (World Bank, 2023, slide 13). By mid-2025, the World Bank's Nigeria Development Update reported that only 5.6 million households, around 37% of the target, had received at least one tranche, and linked the pace to the need for biometric verification of at least one adult per household (World Bank, 2025). By August 2026, a World Bank Implementation Status and Results Report put cumulative coverage at 10.43 million households and 67.19 million people, with 7.1 million households having received all three tranches after biometric verification (World Bank, 2026, cited in ThisDay, 2026). The coverage definitions in these reports may not be identical, so the later figure should be reconciled against the original 15-million target before it is read as a like-for-like measure of progress.

That later progress coincides with unresolved accountability questions from the programme's earlier phase. The Auditor-General for the Federation's 2024 annual report flagged ₦33.75 billion in electronic transfers to 3.3 million households and beneficiaries across 35 states in 2023 for which supporting documentation could not be verified (Auditor-General for the Federation, 2024, cited in ThisDay, 2026). Separately, the then-Minister of Humanitarian Affairs and Poverty Reduction was suspended in early 2024 over an unrelated ₦585 million transfer to a private account, with the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission subsequently recovering approximately ₦32.7 billion and $445,000 in connected funds (reported in national press, 2024). Both findings predate the National Identification Number verification drive the World Bank now credits with improving traceability; whether that drive has resolved the underlying documentation weakness, rather than simply changing it going forward, is not yet independently established.

On poverty, the World Bank's December 2023 projections put the number of poor people in Nigeria at about 104 million in 2023, up from about 95 million in 2021 (World Bank, 2023, slide 12), and its 2025 update put the poverty headcount at 46%, about 40 million more people than in 2019 (World Bank, 2025). On prices, the government's own scorecard reports headline inflation easing from 22.41% in May 2023 to 15.91% in June 2026, and food inflation falling from 24.82% to 17.52% over the same period (Oyedele, 2026). Two qualifications matter. First, the National Bureau of Statistics' own next release, published two days before the Minister's briefing, had already reduced headline inflation further to 15.43%, but recorded food inflation rising to 20.31% — a reversal the scorecard's June figure did not capture (National Bureau of Statistics, 2026d, cited in Proshare, 2026). Second, the Minister himself described household welfare as unfinished business, telling the briefing that “a scorecard that only lists wins is not a scorecard — it is a campaign leaflet, and we did not come here to give you one” (Oyedele, 2026). This brief takes that statement at face value and treats household welfare as an open question rather than a settled gain.

Counter-Arguments

A full assessment should also engage the government's own counter-argument against reversing course. The Minister of Information and National Orientation has argued publicly that restoring the subsidy would recreate the fiscal pressures, fuel scarcity, and market distortions that made the original policy unsustainable, citing the renewed investor confidence, record stock-market performance, and highest foreign reserves in nearly 20 years recorded since the reform (Idris, 2026). This is a serious argument and this brief does not dispute its macroeconomic premise. But the choice is not binary between full subsidy restoration and the status quo. Intermediate options — a smaller, means-tested fuel subsidy targeted at public transport operators; expanding direct cash transfers before any further price adjustment; or simply enforcing existing transparency law before pursuing further reform — are available and are not addressed in either the government's scorecard or, until now, in this brief. Accountability, not reversal, is this brief's recommendation, precisely because the fiscal case for the reform is real even where the household case for its management is not yet proven.

Taken together, the evidence supports neither the government's framing of a clean, delivered success, nor a claim that the reform has failed. It supports two narrower, falsifiable conclusions: the fiscal gains are real but smaller, and more borrowing-dependent, than the headline figure suggests; and the social-protection response, while scaling up, has not yet closed the gap between announced ambition and verified delivery.

What Should Happen Next

By mid-2027, Nigeria should enforce the transparent-reporting obligations it already has in law, extend them explicitly to cover subsidy-reform accounting and cash-transfer delivery, and bring subnational governments into the same standard of disclosure. This does not require new legislation.

1. Enforce the Fiscal Responsibility Act's existing reporting requirement

Section 30 of the Fiscal Responsibility Act 2007 already requires the Minister of Finance to publish quarterly Budget Implementation Reports within 30 days of each quarter's end (Fiscal Responsibility Act, 2007, s.30). An independent analysis found that no quarterly report between 2018 and Q3 2025 was published on time, with delays of 67 to 432 days, and the civic-technology organisation BudgIT reported no report published at all since Q2 2024 (CFG Advisory, cited in BusinessDay, 2026; BudgIT, 2026). The first concrete milestone, achievable within six months, is simply compliance: publication of the overdue reports, and resumption of the statutory quarterly schedule, by the Ministry of Finance and the Budget Office of the Federation. The Fiscal Responsibility Commission, the body legally empowered to monitor compliance and investigate breaches, should be resourced and directed to do so (Fiscal Responsibility Act, 2007).

2. Disaggregate subsidy-reform reporting specifically within that framework

Within twelve months, the Ministry of Finance, drawing Federation Account data from the Office of the Accountant-General of the Federation, should publish quarterly reports in open, machine-readable format that separately identify resources attributed to subsidy removal, resources attributed to foreign-exchange unification, other incremental revenue, and new borrowing, and reconcile all four against audited public accounts rather than press-conference figures. This fills a real gap: Federation Account allocation data are already published monthly, but do not separate these categories, and no reconciliation against audited accounts has been published for 2023–2025. Because the subsidy and FX effects cannot be separated from published totals, the report should state its method — for example, valuing oil and tax receipts at the pre-reform official rate and at the unified rate — and publish the assumptions so that independent analysts can reproduce or challenge the split.

3. Extend the same disclosure standard to state and local governments

States and local governments received almost two-thirds of the ₦15.8 trillion (₦10.4 trillion of ₦15.8 trillion) yet face no comparable public reporting requirement for its use. State Houses of Assembly and State Auditors-General should require and publish annual accounts of FAAC-derived subsidy-linked allocations, modelled on federal reporting once the federal standard above is restored.

4. Measure and report cash-transfer delivery against named, auditable targets

The National Cash Transfer Office should publish quarterly, state-disaggregated figures on eligible households targeted (those on the National Social Register below the programme's ₦75,000-a-month income threshold), households reached, payment timeliness (payment within 10 days of the scheduled date), and unresolved complaints. The Auditor-General for the Federation's documentation concerns from the 2023 tranche should be formally closed out — resolved or explained — before the programme's coverage claims are treated as a settled success. The National Assembly's finance and appropriations committees should receive and review these reports each quarter.

Implementation, cost and risks. None of the above requires a new institution; the Ministry of Finance, the Budget Office of the Federation, the Office of the Accountant-General of the Federation, the Fiscal Responsibility Commission, the Office of the Auditor-General for the Federation, the National Bureau of Statistics, and the National Cash Transfer Office already exist and already have the relevant mandates. Where agencies do not publish, requests under the Freedom of Information Act 2011 give citizens and civil-society organisations a legal route to the underlying data. This brief does not cost the proposals; because they use existing mandates and budget lines, the marginal cost is likely small relative to the sums at stake, but that should be costed before adoption. The main risks are limited capacity to reconcile data across agencies and limited political incentive to publish figures that might complicate a favourable narrative — the same factors behind years of non-compliance with Section 30. The Fiscal Responsibility Commission's own documented history is one of requests “treated with disrespect and unseriousness” by the agencies it oversees (i-scholar, 2015). Incentives would change only if publication deadlines carry consequences, for example public naming of non-compliant quarters by the Commission and National Assembly follow-up on missed reports. BudgIT and CFG Advisory already do the reconciliation work this brief recommends from outside government; a credible first step is for the Ministry of Finance to engage with that scrutiny rather than outpace it.

Measuring Progress by 2030

Progress should be measured against named indicators with baselines, targets, and responsible bodies — not activity alone. The table below (Figure 2) sets out a minimum reporting standard.

Conclusion

The central question this brief set out to answer is whether Nigeria's petrol subsidy reform is working for households, not only for the budget. The honest answer, on the evidence available in October 2026, is: fiscally, in part, with real caveats about borrowing dependence and an unresolved attribution problem; for households, not yet demonstrated, though the delivery infrastructure to demonstrate it either way is now visibly improving.

This assessment rests on government-reported figures, press coverage of a single scorecard presentation, and NBS data that is itself subject to revision; it has no independent counterfactual for what Nigeria's fiscal position would look like without the reform, and it cannot yet draw on an audited reconciliation of any of the headline numbers. These are not reasons to dismiss the government's account — much of it is corroborated across independent sources — but they are reasons to treat it as a first, unaudited account rather than a final one. A short data agenda follows from this: an audited reconciliation of the scorecard; a household-expenditure survey reporting fuel's budget share by zone; and state-level reporting of subsidy-linked allocations.

The most useful next step is not a new round of advocacy for or against the subsidy's removal, but enforcement of the transparency obligations that already exist, extended explicitly to cover this reform's financing and its delivery to households. Nigeria does not need a new accountability framework by 2030; it needs the one it legislated in 2007, applied.
