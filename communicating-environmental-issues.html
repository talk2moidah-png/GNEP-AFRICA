Communicating Environmental Issues: Why Storytelling Matters for Civil Society

GNEP Storytelling and Persuasive Communication Workshop for CSOs in Katsina State. GNEP Africa Documentation

Environmental challenges are often discussed through statistics, technical reports, policy documents and scientific projections. These sources are essential for understanding the scale of environmental problems, but they do not always communicate how those problems affect people's everyday lives. For civil society organisations (CSOs), the challenge is not simply to understand environmental issues. It is also to communicate them in ways that people can relate to, understand and act upon.

Storytelling offers a powerful approach to bridging this gap. By connecting environmental evidence with human experiences, local knowledge and community realities, storytelling can make complex issues more accessible, strengthen public engagement and support meaningful environmental action.

Beyond Facts and Figures

Climate change, deforestation, land degradation, flooding and waste pollution are often presented as broad environmental challenges. Yet their consequences are experienced locally. A farmer may struggle with unpredictable rainfall, a household may face water shortages, and a community may experience the loss of trees that once provided shade, food and protection against environmental pressures.

These experiences provide a human perspective on environmental change. When communicated responsibly, they help audiences understand that environmental problems are not distant scientific concerns but issues connected to livelihoods, health, education, food security and community wellbeing.

This does not mean that stories should replace scientific evidence. Rather, storytelling can complement evidence by showing what statistics mean in people's lives. A report may describe the effects of land degradation, while a community member's experience can illustrate how declining soil quality affects agricultural productivity and household income.

For civil society organisations, combining credible evidence with relatable narratives can make environmental communication more persuasive, memorable and relevant.

Why Storytelling Matters for Civil Society

Civil society organisations often operate at the intersection of communities, public institutions and development partners. They help identify local concerns, raise awareness, mobilise participation and advocate for policy responses. Effective communication is therefore central to their work.

First, storytelling makes complex issues understandable. Environmental policies and scientific concepts can be difficult for audiences without specialised knowledge. Explaining these issues through familiar experiences, local examples and everyday language helps make information more accessible.

Second, storytelling strengthens community participation. People are more likely to engage with environmental initiatives when they recognise their own experiences and concerns in the conversation. Inviting community members to share their perspectives also helps organisations avoid treating local people as passive recipients of information.

Third, storytelling can strengthen advocacy. Decision-makers need reliable evidence to understand environmental challenges and assess possible interventions. Carefully documented community experiences can complement research findings, demonstrate the practical consequences of policy decisions and draw attention to issues that might otherwise receive limited consideration.

Finally, storytelling supports public awareness and collective action. Narratives about community-led tree planting, local conservation efforts or responses to flooding can help audiences understand the value of environmental action and identify opportunities to participate.

These benefits depend on how stories are developed and shared. A compelling narrative should inform audiences, respect the people whose experiences it presents and connect environmental challenges with realistic opportunities for change.

From Community Experiences to Policy Conversations

One of the most valuable contributions civil society can make is to connect local experiences with wider policy discussions.

Environmental challenges are frequently shaped by decisions about land use, public investment, natural resource management, infrastructure and climate adaptation. However, the experiences of affected communities may not always be adequately reflected in formal policy processes.

Storytelling can help bring these experiences into public conversations. For example, a CSO working on climate resilience in northern Nigeria could document how farmers, women, young people and other community members experience changing environmental conditions. With appropriate consent and verification, these accounts could be combined with relevant climate information, research findings and policy analysis.

The resulting communication could take several forms, including a short article, a photo essay, a community radio programme, a social media campaign or a policy brief. Each format serves a different audience, but the central objective remains the same: to communicate credible information in a way that encourages understanding and informed engagement.

For storytelling to contribute meaningfully to advocacy, however, organisations must go beyond describing problems. They should identify the decisions, institutional responsibilities and practical responses relevant to the issues being discussed.

A story about flooding, for instance, can explain the experiences of affected residents while also drawing attention to drainage infrastructure, land-use planning, early-warning systems and local preparedness. This approach connects human experiences with the policy choices that can help address the underlying challenge.

Communicating Responsibly and Ethically

Although storytelling offers significant opportunities, it also carries responsibilities. Environmental narratives can misrepresent communities when they rely on exaggerated claims, oversimplified explanations or images that portray people only as victims.

Civil society organisations must therefore ensure that the stories they tell are accurate, respectful and grounded in evidence.

Obtaining informed consent is particularly important when collecting personal accounts, photographs or videos. Contributors should understand how their stories may be used and shared. Organisations should also protect sensitive information, respect cultural contexts and avoid presenting individual experiences as proof of a wider trend without supporting evidence.

Equally important is the need to avoid exaggeration. Environmental challenges are already serious enough to require attention. Unsupported claims or sensational language may attract temporary interest but can undermine public trust over time.

Responsible storytelling also recognises community agency. People affected by environmental challenges possess knowledge, experience and capacities that can contribute to solutions. Their stories should reflect not only the difficulties they face but also their ideas, initiatives and contributions to environmental protection.

Building Storytelling Capacity Within CSOs

Effective environmental communication does not necessarily require expensive equipment or large communication budgets. It requires clear objectives, careful listening, accurate information and an understanding of the intended audience.

“Civil society organisations can strengthen their storytelling capacity through practical steps:”

Start with the audience. Identify who needs to understand the issue and what information will be most relevant to them.

Listen before communicating. Engage community members and understand their experiences rather than assuming what matters to them.

Combine stories with evidence. Support narratives with verified information, research findings and relevant policy context.

Choose appropriate communication formats. Use articles, photographs, videos, radio, public dialogues or policy briefs according to the audience and purpose.

Develop a clear message. Explain the environmental issue, why it matters, who is affected and what responses are possible.

Evaluate engagement. Assess whether communication activities improve understanding, encourage participation or support constructive dialogue.

Training in writing, public speaking, digital communication and ethical storytelling can help CSOs apply these practices more effectively. Collaboration among researchers, journalists, community leaders and environmental advocates can further strengthen the quality and reach of their messages.

Conclusion: Making Environmental Issues Matter to People

Environmental communication is not simply about distributing information. It is about helping people understand why an issue matters, how it affects their lives and what can be done in response.

For civil society organisations, storytelling provides a practical way to connect scientific evidence with community experiences and policy discussions. When used responsibly, it can make environmental issues more accessible, encourage participation, strengthen advocacy and amplify perspectives that might otherwise remain unheard.

As communities across Nigeria and Africa confront increasingly complex environmental challenges, the ability to communicate clearly and credibly is becoming an important part of effective civil society engagement.

The strongest environmental stories do more than describe a problem. They help people understand its significance, recognise their role in addressing it and see possibilities for change.

About the Author

Maryam DAUDA is a facilitator in storytelling and persuasive communication. Her contribution to civil society capacity development focuses on strengthening the communication skills that organisations need to present ideas clearly, engage audiences and support effective advocacy.

This article is part of GNEP's broader commitment to strengthening civil society capacity for environmental awareness, community engagement and evidence-informed policy advocacy.
